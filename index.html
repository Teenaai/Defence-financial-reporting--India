Research report · November 2026

Defence Financial Reporting Systems: Benchmarking with Global Best Practices and Alignment with Outcome Monitoring and Evaluation

A comparative study of India, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Australia, Canada and France

Executive summary

1. What the study tried to discover

This study set out to answer four questions.

  • Which financial reports on defence do India and other important countries publish?
  • Which reports are common, and which are published abroad but not in India?
  • What information do the common reports contain, and how do India's differ?
  • Which benchmark practices can India adopt, and how can it link its financial reports with outcome monitoring and evaluation, without harming national security?

2. How the research was conducted

1. The official sites of the defence ministry, finance ministry, parliament and audit office of each country were searched for published financial reports.

2. Each report was listed with its exact name, issuing authority and contents, under nine categories of defence financial information (Section 2).

3. The reports were sorted into common reports, extra reports and extra disclosures (Section 3).

4. The contents of the common reports and the extra disclosures were compared side by side (Section 4).

5. Benchmark practices were drawn out (Section 5) and linked with outcome monitoring and evaluation (Section 6).

Seven countries were covered: India, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Australia, Canada and France.

3. What the study found

  • India publishes the basic reports. These are the Demands for Grants 1, the Defence Services Appropriation Accounts 2, the Union Finance Accounts 3 and the CAG audit reports 4.
  • India's reports are narrower. The budget is shown by broad heads, and one head alone holds about 37% of capital outlay. 1 The accounts record cash spent and do not show assets or liabilities. 2
  • India's annual report is not a financial report. The Ministry's Annual Report describes activities. 5 The United Kingdom's annual report contains audited accounts. 6
  • Eight types of report found abroad were not found in India: a ministry balance sheet 7, a formal audit opinion on it 8, a programme-wise budget book 9, a yearly major-projects report 10, contract statistics 11, separate pension accounts 12, a quarterly financial report 13 and a performance report 14.

4. Key insights for India

1. India's system is sound. It needs upgrading, not replacing. India already publishes the budget, the accounts and the audit reports. Every improvement suggested here can be built on these existing reports. 1 2 4

2. Parliament cannot see how much of future defence budgets is already committed. Payments on signed contracts continue for many years. France and Japan publish this figure every year. One total for India would show how much money is really free for new purchases. 15 16

3. Pensions take about one-fifth of the defence budget, yet have no yearly report of their own. The United Kingdom, the United States and Canada publish separate pension reports or accounts. 17 12 18 19

4. India reports what defence spends, but not what the spending achieves. Defence is outside the national outcome framework. Results can be reported first for housing, health care, pensions and works, where nothing is secret. 20

5. More openness need not weaken security. The reports published abroad give totals of money. They do not show the number, location or readiness of forces. 21 15

6. The accounts reach Parliament too late to be useful. India's defence accounts took more than ten months. The United Kingdom takes less than four, so its Parliament can use them for the next budget. 22 6

7. Correct records must come before new reports. The United States publishes the most, yet its auditor has not been able to confirm its figures for eight years. India should move to a statement of assets and liabilities in steps, starting with pensions, land and buildings. 23 24

8. Publishing audit findings openly brings quicker correction. The United Kingdom's defence accounts were criticised by the auditor in one year and found in order the next. 8 25

Three steps possible within one year, using data the Ministry already holds: publish one combined yearly volume of defence finance; place the defence accounts online on the day they are laid in Parliament; and publish one total of payments already committed for future years.

5. Conclusion

India's chain of budget, accounts and audit is sound. The gaps are a joined-up annual financial report, a view of assets and future commitments, regular reports on projects, contracts and pensions, and a link between money and results. Each gap has a safe first step that uses data the Ministry already holds.

1. Introduction

1.1 Meaning of financial reporting

Financial reporting is the way an organisation tells people outside it what money it received, what it spent, what it owns and what it owes. The reports are prepared at fixed times, under fixed rules, so that readers can trust and compare them.

1.2 Corporate and government financial reporting

A company reports mainly to investors, lenders and other creditors, who want to judge its future cash returns. 26 A government reports mainly to citizens and the legislature. Its reports have two purposes: to hold the government to account, and to help people take decisions. 27

Point of difference Corporate reporting Government reporting
Main readers Investors, lenders, creditors 26 Citizens, taxpayers, the legislature 27
Main purpose To help decide whether to invest or lend To show that public money was used lawfully and well
Measure of success Profit Services delivered within the approved budget
Key comparison This year against last year Actual spending against the budget passed by the legislature
Source of money Customers Taxes voted by the legislature
Limit on disclosure Commercial secrecy National security, especially in defence

1.3 Financial reporting in defence

Financial reporting in defence means telling Parliament and the public how much money the armed forces and the defence ministry were given, how it was spent, what was bought, what is owned and what is still to be paid. Defence is harder to report on than other ministries for three reasons. Its assets are huge and hard to value. 28 Its contracts run for many years. And some details must stay secret.

1.4 The financial reporting system

A financial reporting system is everything that stands behind the reports: the offices, the rules, the records, the checks, the reports and the audit. The system covers six stages. If one stage is weak, the final report cannot be trusted.

Figure 1: The six stages of a financial reporting system

1.5 The defence financial reporting system in India

Stage How it works in India
1. Budget The Ministry asks Parliament for money under four Demands for Grants. 1 29
2. Sanction and contract Authorised officers approve spending under delegated financial powers.
3. Payment and recording The Defence Accounts Department pays bills and records each transaction under an account code. 30
4. Internal check The Defence Accounts Department carries out internal audit, and its head, the Controller General of Defence Accounts (CGDA), gives a yearly audit certificate. 31
5. Reports The Ministry prepares the Appropriation Accounts of the Defence Services. 31 32
6. Audit and Parliament The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audits the accounts and reports to Parliament. 4

India's government accounts, including defence, are kept on the cash basis: they record money when it is paid or received. 4 Many other countries use the accrual basis, which also records what is owned and what is owed. An Indian Defence Accounts officer has advised a step-by-step move towards it. 24

1.6 Categories of reports in defence financial reporting

Defence financial information falls into nine categories. One report may cover more than one category.

Category What it covers
1. Budget and allocations Money requested and approved for the year
2. Budget execution and receipts Actual spending against the budget, and money received
3. Procurement and future commitments Programmes, contracts, and payments due in later years
4. Operations and maintenance Cost of running, repairing and supporting the forces
5. Assets and liabilities What defence owns and what it owes
6. Personnel and pensions Pay, allowances, pensions and retirement obligations
7. In-year and long-term view Spending during the year, and affordability of future plans
8. Departmental and government accounts Full accounts of the ministry or of the whole government
9. Audit and results Audit opinions and findings, and results achieved

1.7 Stakeholders of defence financial reports

  • Parliament and its committees approve the money and question how it was used.
  • The Ministry of Defence and the three services use the reports to plan and control spending.
  • The Ministry of Finance uses them to decide future allocations.
  • The auditor (CAG) uses them as the basis of audit.
  • Citizens, taxpayers and the media use them to judge whether public money was well spent.
  • Defence industry and suppliers use them to understand future demand.
  • Researchers and international bodies use them to compare countries. The United Nations asks every state to report its military spending each year. 33

5. Benchmark practices in defence financial reporting

5.1 Meaning of benchmark practices

A benchmark practice is the best known way of doing something, against which others can measure themselves. Benchmarking means comparing one's own practice with it, finding the gap and planning to close it. In financial reporting, benchmark practices relate to four things: the content of the reports, how soon they appear, how reliable they are, and how useful they are to the reader. Benchmarking is not copying. Each country adopts what fits its laws and its security needs.

6. Outcome monitoring and evaluation in defence financial reporting

6.1 Meaning

A financial report says how much was spent. Outcome monitoring means checking regularly what that spending achieved, against targets. Evaluation means asking, afterwards, whether the result was worth the cost. Three words are used. Outlay is the money provided. Output is the direct product, such as houses built. Outcome is the benefit, such as families properly housed. 20 64

Aligning financial reporting with outcome monitoring and evaluation means showing the money, the output and the outcome for the same programme, for the same year, in the same report, and using the findings in the next budget.

7. Conclusion and key insights

India's basic chain of budget, appropriation accounts and audit is sound and matches the other six countries. The comparison shows that India's reports are narrower in content, slower to appear and spread over separate documents. Other countries also publish reports that India does not: a balance sheet, future commitments, major-projects reports, contract statistics, pension accounts, quarterly reports and performance reports. Most of these give totals of money and can be published in a limited form without harming internal security.

Key insights for India

1. Build on what exists. India already publishes the budget, the appropriation accounts and the audit reports. 1 2 4

2. Reliability before volume. The United States publishes the most reports, yet its auditor cannot give an opinion. 23 Records must be correct first.

3. Totals do not reveal secrets. Balance sheets, commitments and pension figures abroad show money, not capability. 21 15 12

4. Publish audit findings openly. The United Kingdom moved from a qualified opinion to a clean one in a year. 8 25

5. Report faster and in one place. A single yearly volume, laid sooner, would serve Parliament better. 6

6. Report the future. A total of committed payments would show the pressure on coming budgets. 15 16

7. Give pensions and contracts their own reports. 12 11

8. Link money with results. Begin with support functions inside the national outcome framework. 20

9. Move to accrual accounts in steps. Start with pensions, land and buildings. 24 63

Limits of the study. The study used public documents only. China and Russia were not covered because their reports could not be verified. Some long documents were checked from official extracts. Every proposal needs review by the competent authorities.

Section 5 of the study

Benchmark practices

A benchmark practice is the best known way of doing something, against which others can measure themselves. Benchmarking means comparing one's own practice with it, finding the gap and planning to close it. In financial reporting, benchmark practices relate to four things: the content of the reports, how soon they appear, how reliable they are, and how useful they are to the reader. Benchmarking is not copying. Each country adopts what fits its laws and its security needs.

Section 6 of the study

Outcome monitoring and evaluation

OutlayMoney provided
OutputDirect product, such as houses built
OutcomeThe benefit, such as families properly housed

A financial report says how much was spent. Outcome monitoring means checking regularly what that spending achieved, against targets. Evaluation means asking, afterwards, whether the result was worth the cost. Three words are used. Outlay is the money provided. Output is the direct product, such as houses built. Outcome is the benefit, such as families properly housed.

Aligning financial reporting with outcome monitoring and evaluation means showing the money, the output and the outcome for the same programme, for the same year, in the same report, and using the findings in the next budget.

Executive summary and Section 7 of the study

Valuable insights for India

India's chain of budget, accounts and audit is sound. The gaps are a joined-up annual financial report, a view of assets and future commitments, regular reports on projects, contracts and pensions, and a link between money and results. Each gap has a safe first step that uses data the Ministry already holds.

Three steps possible within one year, using data the Ministry already holds

  1. Publish one combined yearly volume of defence finance
  2. Place the defence accounts online on the day they are laid in Parliament
  3. Publish one total of payments already committed for future years

Key insights from the executive summary

1

India's system is sound. It needs upgrading, not replacing.

India already publishes the budget, the accounts and the audit reports. Every improvement suggested here can be built on these existing reports.

2

Parliament cannot see how much of future defence budgets is already committed.

Payments on signed contracts continue for many years. France and Japan publish this figure every year. One total for India would show how much money is really free for new purchases.

3

Pensions take about one-fifth of the defence budget, yet have no yearly report of their own.

The United Kingdom, the United States and Canada publish separate pension reports or accounts.

4

India reports what defence spends, but not what the spending achieves.

Defence is outside the national outcome framework. Results can be reported first for housing, health care, pensions and works, where nothing is secret.

5

More openness need not weaken security.

The reports published abroad give totals of money. They do not show the number, location or readiness of forces.

6

The accounts reach Parliament too late to be useful.

India's defence accounts took more than ten months. The United Kingdom takes less than four, so its Parliament can use them for the next budget.

7

Correct records must come before new reports.

The United States publishes the most, yet its auditor has not been able to confirm its figures for eight years. India should move to a statement of assets and liabilities in steps, starting with pensions, land and buildings.

8

Publishing audit findings openly brings quicker correction.

The United Kingdom's defence accounts were criticised by the auditor in one year and found in order the next.

Key insights from the conclusion

India's basic chain of budget, appropriation accounts and audit is sound and matches the other six countries. The comparison shows that India's reports are narrower in content, slower to appear and spread over separate documents. Other countries also publish reports that India does not: a balance sheet, future commitments, major-projects reports, contract statistics, pension accounts, quarterly reports and performance reports. Most of these give totals of money and can be published in a limited form without harming internal security.
